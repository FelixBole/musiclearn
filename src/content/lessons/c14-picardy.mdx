Mixture works in both directions. A major key can borrow from minor (last lesson); a minor key can borrow **major**, most famously on its final chord.

## The Picardy third

A piece in minor that ends on a **major** tonic chord. The raised 3rd turns the final cadence from grief into consolation.

<Progression romans="i iv V7 I" tonic="D" minor label="D minor … ending on D major" />

It was so common in Renaissance and Baroque music that a minor-key piece ending in minor was almost unusual.

## The minor plagal cadence

The reverse borrowing at a cadence: **iv → I** in a major key.

<Progression romans="I IV iv I" label="I – IV – iv – I" />

The borrowed A♭ falls to G while the tonic arrives. It's bittersweet, the "sunset" cadence of film endings.

## Flipping the tonic: I ↔ i

Change only the 3rd of the tonic chord and the whole world changes:

<Progression romans="I IV I i iv i" label="C major … suddenly C minor" />

Same root, same bass, one note lower: E → E♭. This **parallel-mode shift** is one of the most effective dramatic devices there is.

<Composer title="Narrative switches">
- **I → i**: the hero's theme suddenly in minor, the moment something goes wrong. Keep the melody, flip the mode: instant recognition plus a new emotion. It's the core of **leitmotif** writing (more in Track D).
- **Picardy ending**: hope after a sad or tense cue. It's great for a quest-complete stinger in a minor-key area.
- **iv → I**: a gentle, nostalgic close for a scene ending or a menu theme.
