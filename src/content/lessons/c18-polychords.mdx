Stack one chord on top of another and you get a **polychord**. It's a practical way to write rich extended harmony: each layer is a simple triad, easy to orchestrate.

## Upper-structure triads

Put a triad on top of a different bass or chord. The upper triad **implies extensions**:

| Polychord | Upper notes over C | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **D / C** | D F♯ A | Cmaj9♯11 (Lydian, bright, magical) |
| **B♭ / C** | B♭ D F | C11 / C9sus (soft, soul, "open" dominant) |
| **E / C** | E G♯ B | Cmaj7♯5 (strange, luminous) |
| **A♭ / C** | A♭ C E♭ | A♭ over its 3rd (warm, the ♭VI colour) |

Written this way, the voicing is automatically good: **simple and low** (C and G), **colour on top** (the upper triad).

## Bitonality: two triads a tritone apart

C major and F♯ major sounding together is the famous "Petrushka chord" (from Stravinsky's ballet). It's bright and clashing at once: magical chaos, a festival gone wrong.

## Hybrid textures: layering by register

The polychord idea applies to the whole texture:

1. **Low layer**: root and 5th only (synth bass, low strings, a sub layer).
2. **Middle layer**: the triad (brass, choir, pads).
3. **High layer**: the colour (an upper-structure triad, an add9, a high cluster) in violins, flutes, bells or an airy synth.

Each layer stays simple. The richness comes from the stack.

<Composer title="Thinking in layers">
When you orchestrate a dense chord, write it as **two simple chords**: one for the low/middle section (strings, low brass), one for the top (winds, high strings, synth). Each section plays a triad it can tune well, and the combination gives you a 9th, 11th or 13th chord without any section playing an awkward cluster. That's how many big film climaxes get their bright, "expensive" sound.
