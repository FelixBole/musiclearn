As an orchestral composer you will meet two more clefs. Both are **C clefs**: the center of the clef points to the line that is **middle C**.

## Alto clef — the viola

In **alto clef**, middle C is the **middle line**. The viola reads almost exclusively in alto clef, because its range (C3 up to about E6) sits neatly around middle C.

<Example clef="alto" notes="C3 F3 C4 G4 C5" label="C3 – F3 – C4 – G4 – C5" />

Landmarks: **C4** middle line · **F3** bottom line · **G4** top line · **A3** second line · **E4** fourth line.

<Quiz q="In alto clef, the middle line is…" options={["G4", "C4", "B4", "F3"]} answer={1} explain="That is what makes it the alto C clef: the clef's center marks middle C on the middle line." />

## Tenor clef — cello, bassoon, trombone

In **tenor clef**, middle C is the **4th line** (second from top). It is used when cellos, bassoons or trombones play high, to avoid many ledger lines above the bass staff.

<Example clef="tenor" notes="D3 A3 C4 E4" label="D3 – A3 – C4 – E4" />

Landmarks: **C4** 4th line · **A3** 3rd line · **D3** bottom line · **E4** top line.

<Callout title="A shortcut">
Compare with treble clef at the **same staff position**:
- **Alto**: the letter is one step **higher** than in treble (treble bottom line E → alto bottom line F), sounding a 7th lower.
- **Tenor**: the letter is one step **lower** than in treble (treble bottom line E → tenor bottom line D), sounding a 9th lower.

It helps at first, but landmarks plus daily drilling win in the long run.
</Callout>

<Composer title="Writing for violas">
In your DAW you might write viola lines in treble clef — but players read alto clef, and many score-reading resources show it. Being able to read viola parts lets you study scores of film and game music, where violas often carry the harmony's inner voice (the 3rd or 7th of the chord) — exactly the notes that make a voicing sound rich.
</Composer>
