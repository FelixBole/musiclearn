Open a horn part and you'll see a G where the orchestra plays a C. Several instruments are **transposing**: the note written in their part is not the note that sounds. It's a historical convenience, so a player can switch between instruments of the same family with the same fingerings.

## The rule

An instrument **"in X"** sounds **X** when it plays a written **C**.

- **Clarinet in B♭**: written C → sounds B♭ (a major 2nd lower). To get concert C, write **D**.
- **Trumpet in B♭**: the same.
- **Horn in F**: written C → sounds F below (a perfect 5th lower). To get concert C, write **G**.
- **Clarinet in A**: sounds a minor 3rd lower. To get concert C, write **E♭**.

So the part is always written **higher** than the sound, by the interval between C and the instrument's key.

| Instrument | Sounds… | Concert C is written as |
|---|---|---|
| Clarinet in B♭, trumpet in B♭ | a major 2nd lower | D |
| Horn in F, English horn | a perfect 5th lower | G |
| Clarinet in A | a minor 3rd lower | E♭ |
| Alto sax (in E♭) | a major 6th lower | A |
| Piccolo | an octave **higher** | C an octave lower |
| Double bass | an octave lower | C an octave higher |

## Key signatures transpose too

A B♭ clarinet part for a piece in C major is written in **D major** (two sharps). A horn part in F for C major would be in G major. In practice, many modern horn parts are written without key signature, with accidentals.

<Callout title="In your DAW">
Write and think in **concert pitch** (most DAWs and notation programs have a "concert pitch" view). Transposition only matters when you print parts for real players, and the notation software does it for you. But you must be able to **read** a transposed part, and know where the instrument's range lies in written terms.
</Callout>
