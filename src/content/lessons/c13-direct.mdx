Pivot chords are smooth. Sometimes you want the opposite: a key change the listener **notices**. There are two classic ways.

## Common-tone modulation

Hold **one note** while the harmony shifts around it into a distant key. The held note is the thread the ear follows, so even a remote key feels connected.

C major → A♭ major, holding C:

<Progression romans="I IV I Ab: I IV V7 I" label="C … then A♭: C is held into the new tonic" />

C major → E major, holding E:

<Progression romans="I V I E: I IV V7 I" label="C → E through the common tone E" />

Both destinations are **chromatic mediants** of C (roots a third away, one shared note). You'll meet that relationship again in the cinematic harmony unit. It's the same sound, used as a modulation.

## Direct (phrase) modulation

No preparation: a phrase ends, the next one simply starts in the new key. It works best at a phrase boundary, after a cadence, a pause or a drum fill.

The famous pop version is the **"truck-driver" modulation**: repeat the last section a step higher for an energy boost. Inserting the new key's V7 first smooths it:

<Progression romans="I IV V I D: V7 I IV V I" label="C … then up a step to D (via A7)" />

<Callout type="warn" title="Use it once">
The step-up modulation is effective the first time and tiresome after that. It also signals "final chorus", so save it for an ending.
</Callout>

<Composer title="Key changes in game music">
- **Common-tone modulation** is perfect for **area transitions** and reveals. Sustain the shared note in high strings or a synth pad while everything else changes: a new place, still connected.
- **Direct modulation** suits **state changes**: combat starts, a boss phase changes. Up a half or whole step = more urgency.
- Loops must come back: if a cue modulates, plan how the loop returns to its starting key (a pivot or a dominant of the home key just before the loop point).
</Composer>
