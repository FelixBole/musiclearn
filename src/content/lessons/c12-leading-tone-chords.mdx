The dominant has a sibling: the **leading-tone chord**. Just as vii°7 can replace V7 in a key, **vii°7/x** can replace V7/x before any target chord.

## vii°7/x

Build a fully diminished 7th on the leading tone of the target. Before G (V in C), the leading tone is F♯:

All four notes sit a half or whole step from a note of the target. That makes the diminished 7th sound like it **melts** into its resolution. It's more tense and more dramatic than V7/x.

## The symmetry of the diminished 7th

A diminished 7th is a stack of **minor 3rds** that divides the octave into four equal parts (C♯ E G B♭ → C♯). Every inversion is another diminished 7th, just respelled. So:

- There are only **three** different diminished 7th sounds.
- Each one can resolve to **four** different targets, depending on how you spell it.

This makes the dim7 a universal pivot. In 19th-century opera it was the "trapdoor" into any key.

## Tonicization vs modulation

A V/x or vii°7/x **tonicizes** its target: it treats it as a tonic for a moment, and then we're back in the home key. If the new tonic is confirmed by a **cadence** and the music stays there, it's a **modulation** (next unit).

The diminished 7th is so dramatic that it became a cliché (the silent-movie villain). Two ways to use it freshly:

- As a **passing chord**, quick and soft, in the middle of a string line.
- Over a **pedal** (a held bass note), as tension that resolves back to the chord above the same bass.
