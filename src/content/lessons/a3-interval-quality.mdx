Size is the letter count; **quality** is the exact number of half steps. C–E and C–E♭ are both 3rds, but sound very different.

## The two families

- **Perfect** family: unison, **4th**, **5th**, octave — *perfect*, or *augmented* (+1 half step) / *diminished* (−1)
- **Major/minor** family: **2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th** — *major*, *minor* (1 half step smaller), plus augmented / diminished at the extremes

| Interval | Half steps | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| minor 2nd | 1 | sharp tension (Jaws) |
| Major 2nd | 2 | step, neutral |
| minor 3rd | 3 | sad / dark |
| Major 3rd | 4 | bright / happy |
| Perfect 4th | 5 | open, hymn-like |
| Tritone (A4/d5) | 6 | unstable, ominous |
| Perfect 5th | 7 | open, powerful |
| minor 6th | 8 | bittersweet, yearning |
| Major 6th | 9 | warm, sweet |
| minor 7th | 10 | bluesy tension |
| Major 7th | 11 | lush, sharp |
| Octave | 12 | same note, higher |

## The major-scale trick

From the lower note, imagine its **major scale**: the 2nd, 3rd, 6th and 7th of that scale are **major**; the 4th, 5th and octave are **perfect**. One half step smaller than major = minor.

Example: D → F. D major has F♯, so D–F♯ = Major 3rd ⇒ **D–F = minor 3rd**.

## Inversion

Flip an interval (move the bottom note up an octave): the sizes **add up to 9**, and qualities flip — major ↔ minor, augmented ↔ diminished, perfect stays perfect.
So a Major 3rd inverts to a minor 6th; a Perfect 4th to a Perfect 5th.

## Consonance and dissonance

**Consonant** (stable): unison, 3rds, 6ths, perfect 5th, octave. **Dissonant** (wants to move): 2nds, 7ths, tritone. The 4th is a special case — consonant in melody, treated as dissonant above the bass in classical harmony.
