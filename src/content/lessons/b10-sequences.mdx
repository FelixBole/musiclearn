A **sequence** repeats a short harmonic (and melodic) pattern, each time shifted by the same interval. Once it starts, the listener's ear predicts it — so sequences create momentum almost for free.

## Descending fifths

Roots fall by 5ths (or rise by 4ths) through **every** diatonic chord:

<Progression romans="I IV vii° iii vi ii V I" label="I – IV – vii° – iii – vi – ii – V – I" />

You know this from countless Baroque pieces and jazz standards (“Autumn Leaves” is built on it). It's the circle of fifths, staying inside one key.

## The Pachelbel sequence

Down a 4th, up a step, repeated:

<Progression romans="I V vi iii IV I IV V" label="I – V – vi – iii – IV – I – IV – V" />

The bass line (D–A–B–F♯–G–D–G–A in Pachelbel's key) is one of the most reused patterns in music — from wedding canons to rock ballads and RPG town themes.

## Ascending 5–6

Over a bass rising by step, the upper voices alternate a 5th and a 6th above the bass, climbing:

<Progression romans="I vi6 ii vii°6 iii I6 IV" label="Ascending 5–6" />

Rising sequences build **energy** — ideal for a build-up into a climax.

<Composer title="Sequences for builds and transitions">
Need to get from a calm section to a battle in 8 bars? An **ascending sequence** (or a melodic idea repeated a step higher each time) with a crescendo does it convincingly. Need a cue to feel like it's “going somewhere” while staying in one key? **Descending fifths**. Epic trailer music often stacks an ascending sequence with rising percussion intensity — the same technique Vivaldi used.
