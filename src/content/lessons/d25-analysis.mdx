The fastest way to grow as a composer is to **analyse** music you love and steal its techniques, not its notes. Here's a template you can use on any cue, from a film score or a game OST.

## The template

Listen several times, each time focusing on one row. Write short answers.

| # | Question | What to note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Function & mood** | What is the scene/gameplay? What should the player feel? |
| 2 | **Tempo & meter** | BPM, time signature, steady or changing? |
| 3 | **Key & mode** | Tonic, major/minor/modal colour (characteristic note?) |
| 4 | **Harmony** | Bass line first, then the chords. Loop? Borrowed chords, mediants, pedals? |
| 5 | **Harmonic rhythm** | How often do chords change? Speeding up toward climaxes? |
| 6 | **Motifs** | Main motif (rhythm + contour). How is it transformed? |
| 7 | **Layers** | Melody, countermelody, pad, bass, ostinato: who plays what, in which register? |
| 8 | **Orchestration** | Which sections, doublings, special techniques (tremolo, pizzicato, swells)? |
| 9 | **Form** | Sections (A, B, build, climax), loop point, transitions. |
| 10 | **Dynamics curve** | Sketch the intensity over time: where's the peak? |

## Finding the harmony quickly

1. Hum or play the **bass line** first: it usually gives the chord roots.
2. Decide **major or minor** for each chord (listen for the 3rd above the bass).
3. Type what you found into the [progression playground](#/tools/playground) and compare it with the recording. If it sounds wrong, check the bass again.
4. Look for familiar patterns from this course: i–VI–III–VII, ♭VI–♭VII–I, chromatic mediants, pedals.

## Turning analysis into technique

End each analysis with **one sentence per technique you want to reuse**:

- *"The battle theme keeps a D pedal in the low strings and changes chords above it (Dm, B♭/D, C/D)."*
- *"The town theme uses I–IV–♭VII–IV in Mixolydian with a solo flute melody."*
- *"The boss theme's climax is the hero motif in augmentation, horns + trombones, over a diminution ostinato in the strings."*

Then try each technique in a 16-bar sketch of your own. That's how analysis becomes skill.
