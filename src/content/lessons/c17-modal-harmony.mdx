Write a Dorian melody, harmonize it with a V7 → i cadence, and it suddenly sounds like plain minor. Modes are fragile: the **leading tone** of major/minor pulls them back. Here's how to keep them alive.

## Rule 1: avoid the leading tone, use modal cadences

Replace V → I with the mode's own characteristic move:

| Mode | Cadence | In… |
|---|---|---|
| Mixolydian | ♭VII → I | G: F → G |
| Dorian | IV → i | D: G → Dm |
| Aeolian | ♭VI → ♭VII → i | A: F → G → Am |
| Phrygian | ♭II → i | E: F → Em |
| Lydian | II → I | F: G → F |

## Rule 2: anchor the tonic with a pedal

A **pedal point** is a note held (usually in the bass) while the harmony changes above it. The constant bass keeps telling the ear where home is, so modal chords can't drift into another key.

<Progression romans="Dm G/D Dm C/D Dm" tonic="D" minor keySig="C" label="D pedal: Dm – G/D – Dm – C/D – Dm" />

C/D is a C major chord over a D that isn't part of it. The friction is part of the colour.

<Progression romans="Em F/E Em" tonic="E" minor keySig="C" label="E Phrygian over an E pedal" />

## Rule 3: let an ostinato carry the mode

An **ostinato** is a short pattern repeated over and over. A tonic-based ostinato does what a pedal does, with rhythm and energy:

<Example notes="D3 A3 D4 A3 D3 A3 E4 A3 D3 A3 F4 A3 D3 A3 E4 A3" label="Ostinato on D" />

Put chords over it: Dm, G, C, Dm. The ostinato never changes, the harmony glides on top, and the mode stays clear.

Pedal + ostinato is **the** foundation of loopable game music:

- It's **stable**: you can add or remove layers on top (vertical layering) without harmonic clashes.
- It **hides the loop point**: no cadence means no "ending" for the ear to notice.
- It's **flexible**: change the chords above a fixed ostinato to raise tension without breaking the loop.
