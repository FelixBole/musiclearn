Good voice leading is about **independence**: each voice should sound like its own line, not a shadow of another.

## Four kinds of motion

Between any two voices:

- **Parallel**: same direction, same interval (3rds → 3rds)
- **Similar**: same direction, different intervals
- **Contrary**: opposite directions — the most independent
- **Oblique**: one voice holds, the other moves

Between the **bass and soprano** (the outer voices, the ones listeners hear most), aim for plenty of **contrary** and **oblique** motion.

## The famous rule: no parallel 5ths or octaves

When two voices move in parallel **perfect 5ths** or **octaves**, they blend so well that they momentarily sound like *one* voice. In four-part writing, that's a loss of a part — so it's avoided.

Parallel **3rds and 6ths** are fine — they're the sweetest intervals in music.

## Direct (hidden) 5ths and octaves

A softer rule for the **outer voices**: don't move in the *same direction* into a 5th or octave with a **leap in the soprano**. The soprano stepping into it is fine.

<Composer title="Breaking the rules on purpose">
Parallel 5ths and octaves are banned in *part writing* — but orchestration is different. **Doubling** a melody in octaves (violins + cellos) is one line made thicker, not two independent voices. And deliberate **parallel 5ths** (“power chords”, organum) give a raw, ancient, heroic feel — think of brass in open 5ths moving in parallel under a fantasy title theme. Know the rule so that when you break it, it's a choice.
</Composer>
