The **augmented 6th chords** are the most intense pre-dominants in tonal music. They're built around one interval: the **augmented 6th** between ♭6 in the bass and ♯4 above. The two notes are each a half step from the dominant, and they **expand outward** onto it.

In C: A♭ (bass) and F♯. A♭ falls to G, F♯ rises to G: an octave on the dominant.

## Three nationalities

The names are traditional (they don't mean much). All three contain ♭6, 1 and ♯4:

| Chord | In C | Added note |
|---|---|---|
| **Italian (It⁺⁶)** | A♭ – C – F♯ | (none: double the tonic) |
| **French (Fr⁺⁶)** | A♭ – C – D – F♯ | 2nd degree |
| **German (Ger⁺⁶)** | A♭ – C – E♭ – F♯ | ♭3 |

## The German 6th, and those parallel fifths

The German 6th has a problem: going straight to V creates **parallel 5ths** (♭6–♭3 moving to 5–2). The standard fix is to pass through the **cadential 6/4**:

<Progression romans="I Ger+6 I64 V7 I" label="Ger⁺⁶ → I⁶₄ → V7 → I" />

<Progression romans="i Ger+6 i64 V i" tonic="A" minor label="In minor" />

<Callout title="The German 6th is a disguised dominant 7th">
A♭ C E♭ F♯ sounds exactly like **A♭7** (A♭ C E♭ G♭). Only the spelling and the resolution differ. Composers use this as a trapdoor: play A♭7, then resolve it as an augmented 6th (to G) or as a dominant (to D♭). It's also the same idea as the jazz **tritone substitution**.
</Callout>
