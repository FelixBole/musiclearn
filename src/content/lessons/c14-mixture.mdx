If you've ever wondered why film and game music sounds *epic* rather than simply happy or sad, this lesson is a big part of the answer. **Modal mixture** means borrowing chords from the **parallel** key: C minor chords inside C major.

## Where the borrowed chords come from

C major and C minor share a tonic but differ in three notes: **E♭, A♭, B♭** (♭3, ♭6, ♭7). Every chord that uses one of them is a borrowed chord:

| Borrowed chord | In C major | Borrowed note |
|---|---|---|
| **iv** | F minor | A♭ |
| **♭VI** | A♭ major | A♭, E♭ |
| **♭VII** | B♭ major | B♭ |
| **♭III** | E♭ major | E♭, B♭ |
| **ii°** / **iiø7** | D F A♭ (C) | A♭ |

The ♭ in "♭VI" means the root is lowered compared to the major scale.

## Minor iv: the shadow

<Progression romans="I IV iv I" label="I – IV – iv – I: the 6th drops A → A♭" />

Only one note changes (A → A♭) and the mood darkens instantly. It's wistful and nostalgic. Notice how it resolves: A♭ → G, a half step down.

## ♭VI and ♭VII: the epic pair

<Progression romans="I bVI bVII I" label="I – ♭VI – ♭VII – I" />

Two big major chords borrowed from minor, climbing by whole step into the major tonic. It's triumphant and heroic: the **"victory cadence"** of countless games and adventure films. No leading tone is involved; the strength comes from the stepwise roots and the bright-but-dark major chords.

<Callout title="Borrowed chords keep their function">
iv and iiø7 are still pre-dominants, and ♭VI usually is too. You're not changing the harmonic road, just the colour of the paving.

<Composer title="The heart of the epic sound">
Much of what people call "epic" is major-key music full of **borrowed** chords: ♭VI, ♭VII, iv, and in minor keys the mirror image (the major VI, III, VII of natural minor). Some recipes:

- **Heroic theme**: I – ♭VII – IV – I (brass, open voicings).
- **Victory**: … ♭VI – ♭VII – I with a big crescendo.
- **Nostalgia / loss**: I – IV – iv – I (strings, soft).
