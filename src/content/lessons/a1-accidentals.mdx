## Half steps and whole steps

The smallest distance in Western music is the **half step** (semitone): any key to the very next key on the piano, black or white. Two half steps make a **whole step** (tone).

<KeyboardDemo highlight="E4 F4 B4 C5" from="C4" to="C5" />

Between most white keys there is a black key — so they are a whole step apart. But **E–F** and **B–C** have no black key between them: they are **natural half steps**. This tiny fact explains the shape of every scale.

## Accidentals

- **♯ sharp** raises a note a half step
- **♭ flat** lowers a half step
- **♮ natural** cancels a sharp/flat
- **𝄪 double sharp** / **𝄫 double flat** — two half steps (they appear in keys like G♯ minor, or in chromatic harmony)

An accidental lasts **until the end of the bar**, for notes on that same line or space. The barline cancels it.

## Enharmonic equivalents

The same piano key can have several names: C♯ = D♭, F♯ = G♭, even E♯ = F and C♭ = B. These are **enharmonic** spellings. They *sound* the same but *mean* different things in a key — later, spelling correctly (e.g. the 3rd of a B major chord is D♯, not E♭) will make harmony far easier to read.

## Octave names: C4 and your DAW

To say *which* C, we number octaves: **scientific pitch notation** starts each octave at C. **Middle C = C4**, the A above it = A4 (440 Hz). The piano spans A0 to C8.

<Callout type="warn" title="Your DAW might disagree">
Many DAWs and sample libraries label middle C as **C3** (Ableton, Cubase and Logic by default, many Kontakt libraries), and some as **C5** (FL Studio). This app always uses **C4 = middle C**. When you read ranges in orchestration books, they use C4 too — convert carefully when you set keyswitches or check ranges in your DAW.
</Callout>
