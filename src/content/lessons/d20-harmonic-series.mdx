Why does a chord on a full orchestra sometimes sound glorious and sometimes like mud, with the *same notes*? The answer is **spacing**, and nature gives us the model: the **harmonic series**.

## The harmonic series

Every pitched note contains a stack of overtones above its fundamental. On C2:

<Example notes="C2 C3 G3 C4 E4 G4 Bb4 C5" clef="grand" label="C2 and its first overtones" />

Look at the intervals from the bottom: **octave, 5th, 4th, major 3rd, minor 3rd, minor 3rd, major 2nd…** They get **smaller as you go up**. Our ears are used to this shape, so chords spaced the same way sound full, resonant and clear.

## The rule

> Wide intervals at the bottom, close intervals at the top.

- In the **bass register**: octaves and 5ths only.
- In the **middle**: 4ths, 5ths, 6ths, 3rds.
- At the **top**: 3rds and 2nds are fine.

## Low interval limits

Every interval has a pitch below which it turns muddy (the overtones of the two notes clash). Arrangers use these approximate limits for the **lower** note:

| Interval | Lowest clear position |
|---|---|
| minor 2nd | E3 |
| major 2nd | E♭3 |
| minor 3rd | C3 |
| major 3rd | B♭2 |
| perfect 4th | A2 |
| perfect 5th | B♭1 |
| 6ths and 7ths | F2 |

So a C major triad in close position is fine from about C3 up, but below that, open it up.

<Composer title="Fixing mud in the DAW">
When an orchestral mockup sounds muddy, don't reach for the EQ first: look at the **voicing**. Usually the culprit is a 3rd or a cluster of notes between C2 and C3 (often in low brass, low strings or a piano pad doubling everything). Move those notes up an octave, or leave only roots and 5ths down there. The mix clears up immediately. The [chord identifier](#/tools) runs this same check on whatever you play on your MIDI keyboard.
