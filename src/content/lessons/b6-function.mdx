You know the chords of a key. Now the big question: **why does one chord want to go to another?** The answer is *function*. Chords group into three families, each with a job.

## The three families

| Function | Chords (major) | Job | Feeling |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tonic (T)** | **I**, vi, (iii) | home, rest | stable |
| **Predominant (PD)** | **IV**, **ii** | leaving home, preparing | motion |
| **Dominant (D)** | **V**, vii° | tension that demands home | pull |

Chords in the same family share notes: IV and ii share F and A; V and vii° share B, D and F. That's why they can stand in for each other.

## The cycle

Tonal harmony moves around this loop:

**T → PD → D → T**

Every one of these is the same story told with different chords: home, leave, tension, home. You can extend the tonic area (I → vi), or the predominant area (IV → ii), before moving on.

## Root motion: why the cycle works

The strongest root movements are:

- **down a 5th** (V → I, ii → V, vi → ii): the engine of tonal music
- **down a 3rd** (I → vi, vi → IV): gentle, shares two notes
- **up a step** (IV → V, V → vi): fresh, no shared notes

Going *backwards* — **V → IV** or **V → ii** — is called a **retrogression**. Classical style avoids it. Rock and film music use it freely (the “Mixolydian” V–IV–I of countless anthems) — but you should know you're doing it.
