Second-inversion chords (**6/4**, the 5th in the bass) are unstable — the 4th above the bass sounds like a dissonance wanting to resolve. In classical style they appear in just **three** situations. Learn these and you'll recognise them everywhere.

## 1. Cadential 6/4: I64 → V

The most important one. Over the dominant bass note, the upper voices first form a C major chord (I64), then fall to the real V: **6 → 5** and **4 → 3** above the bass.

<Progression romans="I ii6 I64 V I" label="Cadential 6/4" />

Although it's spelled like I, it **functions as dominant** — it's a decorated V. That's why it's usually analysed as “V⁶₄ ⁵₃” in many textbooks.

## 2. Passing 6/4: I – V64 – I6

The bass passes by step between two positions of the same chord; the 6/4 is a by-product of the motion.

<Progression romans="I V64 I6 IV V I" label="Passing 6/4" />

## 3. Pedal (neighbour) 6/4: I – IV64 – I

The bass **holds** while the upper voices move to neighbour notes and back.

<Progression romans="I IV64 I V I" label="Pedal 6/4" />

## The rule

In a 6/4 chord, **double the bass** (the 5th of the chord) and approach/leave it smoothly. A random 6/4 chord out of nowhere sounds like a mistake.

<Composer title="The cadential 6/4 as a dramatic device">
Film and game scores love the cadential 6/4 at climaxes: the orchestra hits a huge tonic-sounding chord over the dominant bass (I64), holds it — cymbal swell — and only then falls to V and home. It stretches the final arrival of a victory theme for maximum payoff.
