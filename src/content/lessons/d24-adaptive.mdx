Adaptive (interactive) music changes with gameplay. Almost every technique is one of two kinds, or a mix of both.

## Vertical layering (re-orchestration)

One piece of music is split into **synchronized stems** that always play together; the game fades layers in and out according to intensity.

<Layers romans="i VI III VII" tonic="D" minor melody="D5:h. A4:q Bb4:h. D5:q C5:q A4:q F4:h E4:h G4:h" />

Try the **Explore → Tension → Combat** presets: the music never stops or jumps, it just gets denser.

How to write for it:

- **Every combination** of layers must sound complete and balanced, including just one layer.
- Same tempo, key and harmony for all layers; each layer in its **own register** (unit 21).
- Put the **foundation** (pad or bass) in the lowest intensity layer; the most exciting material (percussion, melody, brass) in the top layers.
- Avoid a layer that only makes sense with another one (e.g. a countermelody without its melody).

## Horizontal resequencing

The music is cut into **sections** (A, B, combat, victory…) and the game jumps between them at **musical boundaries** (the next bar or phrase), often via short **transition** pieces.

- Write sections with **compatible keys and tempos**, or write transitions that modulate.
- Mark **exit points** (bar lines, phrase ends) where a switch sounds natural.
- Short **stingers** can cover a switch (a cymbal swell or a brass hit).

## Mixing both

Most modern game scores combine them: horizontal sections for big state changes (exploration → boss fight), vertical layers inside each section for intensity, and stingers for events. Middleware such as FMOD and Wwise (both integrate with Unity) is built around exactly these concepts: **tracks** for vertical layers, **transition markers and regions** for horizontal moves.
