The most expressive dissonances are the ones that **delay** a resolution. Film music is built on them.

## Suspensions

A **suspension** holds a note from one chord into the next, where it becomes dissonant, then **resolves down by step**. Three stages:

1. **Preparation** — the note is a consonant chord tone
2. **Suspension** — the chord changes under it; now it's dissonant
3. **Resolution** — it steps down to a chord tone

<Chorale notes="C3,G3,E4,C5 G2,G3,D4,C5 G2,G3,D4,B4 C3,G3,E4,C5" romans="I V(4) V(3) I" label="4–3 suspension: C held over G, resolves to B" />

Named by intervals above the bass: **4–3**, **7–6**, **9–8** (and **2–3** when the bass itself is suspended).

## Appoggiatura

An **accented** dissonance **approached by leap** and resolved by step — the musical “sigh”. More urgent than a suspension because it isn't prepared.

<Example notes="C5 A5 G5" label="Leap to A (dissonant), fall to G" />

## Anticipation

A note of the **next** chord arrives early — common in cadences (the soprano moves to the tonic just before the final chord).

## Pedal point

A note — usually in the bass — **held through changing harmonies**. A **tonic pedal** grounds the music; a **dominant pedal** builds huge tension before a return.

<Composer title="Sus chords, pedals and the cinematic sound">
The pop “sus4” chord is a suspension that never resolves. Held 4ths and 9ths over a **pedal** — strings sustaining while the harmony shifts above a low drone — are the backbone of epic and ambient scoring. A **dominant pedal** under a building ostinato is the textbook way to make players feel “something is coming” before a boss appears.
