Film music follows the picture. Game music follows the **player**, who might stay in one area for ten minutes or leave after ten seconds. Two basic tools: **loops** that can repeat without wearing out, and **stingers** that react to events.

## Loops

A loop must:

1. **Reconnect seamlessly**: the last bar must lead naturally into the first.
2. **Not end too strongly**: a perfect authentic cadence at the loop point sounds like an ending every time it repeats.
3. **Survive repetition**: vary orchestration across the loop, avoid one unforgettable "hook" moment that becomes annoying on the 20th hearing.

Harmonically, end the loop on a chord that **leads back** to the opening chord:

<Progression romans="I vi IV V" tonic="G" label="Loop: V leads back into I" />

<Progression romans="I IV bVII IV" tonic="D" label="Mixolydian loop: no leading tone at all" />

<Callout type="warn" title="The loop-point trap">
Sustained notes and **reverb tails** get cut when the file loops. Plan for it: end with short notes or a breath, or bounce the tail of the last bar onto the first bar of the file (most audio middleware also supports tail regions).
</Callout>

## Stingers

A **stinger** is a short cue (1–5 seconds) triggered by an event. It should be instantly readable and **in the key of the music under it** (or keyless, like a percussion hit), so it doesn't clash with the playing loop.

<Composer title="A stinger kit per area">
For each area of the game, write the loop and a small kit of stingers **in the same key**: discovery, danger, success, failure. Matching keys means the game engine can fire them at any moment without clashes. And reuse your motifs: a stinger that quotes the hero motif in a fragment makes the world feel coherent.
</Composer>
