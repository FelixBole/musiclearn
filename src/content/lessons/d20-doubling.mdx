An orchestral chord often has 15 or 20 notes, but only 3 or 4 different pitch classes. Everything else is **doubling**. What you double, and where, decides whether a tutti sounds bright and powerful or thick and dull.

## What to double

In order of safety:

1. **The root**: in several octaves, especially low. It's the strongest, most stable note.
2. **The 5th**: adds body without colour.
3. **The 3rd**: it gives the chord its major/minor identity, so one or two copies **in the upper register** are enough. A doubled 3rd low sounds thick and dull.
4. **Never double** the leading tone or the 7th of a dominant (they have to resolve; doubling them creates parallel octaves, just as in four-part writing).

## Octave doublings: power without mud

Doubling a **melody** in octaves adds power and presence without thickening the harmony. Classic pairs:

- Violins I + flutes **an octave higher**: brilliant.
- Violins + violas in octaves: a full, warm string melody.
- Cellos + horns in **unison**: heroic and rich.
- Cellos + basses in octaves: the standard bass line.

## Keeping a tutti clear

- Give each **layer its own register**: the melody shouldn't sit in the middle of the pad.
- The **low-mid register (C3–C4)** fills up fastest: low brass, violas, cellos, left-hand piano and synth pads all live there. Thin it out first.
- If two sections play the same notes in the same octave, ask whether you need both. Doubling in **unison** blends colours; doubling in **octaves** adds power.
