You already hear music fluently. This track is about **reading** it just as fluently, so that a score or a piano roll turns into sound in your head, and harmony becomes something you can plan rather than search for.

## The staff

Music is written on a **staff** of five lines and four spaces. Higher on the staff = higher pitch. Notes sit either **on a line** (the line runs through the notehead) or **in a space**.

Each step up the staff (line → space → line) moves up **one letter name**: A B C D E F G, then A again.

## The treble clef (G clef)

The **treble clef** curls around the second line from the bottom and declares: *this line is G* (the G above middle C, G4). Every other note follows from there.

<Example notes="E4 G4 B4 D5 F5" label="Lines: E G B D F" />

- **Lines** (bottom → top): **E G B D F** — *Every Good Boy Does Fine*
- **Spaces** (bottom → top): **F A C E** — spells *FACE*

## Middle C and ledger lines

Notes beyond the staff get short **ledger lines**. The most important one: **middle C (C4)** sits on one ledger line *below* the treble staff. It is the C nearest the middle of a piano, and the meeting point between treble and bass clefs.

<Example notes="C4 D4 E4" label="C4 – D4 – E4" />

<Callout title="Read by landmarks, not by counting">
Counting up from the bottom line every time is slow. Memorize a few **landmarks** — G4 (2nd line), middle C (below), C5 (3rd space), G5 (above the top line) — and read nearby notes as *one step from a landmark*. That is how fast readers do it.
</Callout>

<Composer title="Who reads treble clef?">
Violins, flutes, oboes, clarinets, trumpets, horns (mostly), the right hand of piano/harp/celesta, and high voices. When you write a violin line in your DAW, its notation is in treble clef — reading it fluently lets you check ranges and voice-leading at a glance.
</Composer>
