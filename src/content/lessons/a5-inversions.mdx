A chord is **inverted** when a note other than the root is in the **bass** (the lowest note). The upper notes can be in any order — only the bass decides the inversion.

| Position | Bass note | Figured bass | Slash chord |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root position | root | (5/3) | C |
| 1st inversion | 3rd | **6** | C/E |
| 2nd inversion | 5th | **6/4** | C/G |

## Figured bass in one minute

The numbers describe **intervals above the bass**. In first inversion E–G–C, above E we find a 3rd (G) and a **6th** (C) → “6”. In second inversion G–C–E: a **6th** (E) and a **4th** (C) → “6/4”. Roman-numeral analysis borrows these: **I6**, **V6**, **I64**.

## Why inversions matter

- **Smooth bass lines.** C – G/B – Am – Am/G – F… the bass walks down by step instead of jumping.
- **Weight control.** Root position = solid and final; 1st inversion = lighter, “in motion”; 2nd inversion = unstable (used in specific ways, see Track B).

<Composer title="Descending bass lines = instant emotion">
The stepwise falling bass (often with inversions on top) is behind countless emotional cues. In orchestral writing, give it to cellos and basses in octaves, keep the upper voices moving as little as possible, and you get an effect like 'Lament' or 'Farewell' almost for free.
