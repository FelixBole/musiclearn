Rather than writing the same sharps and flats on every note, music puts them once at the start of each line: the **key signature**.

## Order of sharps and flats

Sharps always appear in the order **F C G D A E B**, flats in the reverse order **B E A D G C F**.

<Example keySig="Ab" notes="Ab4 Bb4 C5 Db5 Eb5 F5 G5 Ab5" label="A♭ major — 4 flats" />

## Reading the key instantly

- **Sharps**: the last sharp is the leading tone (degree 7) → the key is a **half step above** it. F♯ C♯ G♯ → **A major**.
- **Flats**: the **second-to-last flat** is the key. B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ → **A♭ major**. One flat (B♭) = **F major** (memorize).
- No sharps or flats: **C major** (or A minor).

## The circle of fifths

Arrange the keys so each step clockwise goes **up a perfect 5th** and adds one sharp: C – G – D – A – E – B – F♯ – C♯. Counter-clockwise goes up a 4th (down a 5th) and adds one flat: C – F – B♭ – E♭ – A♭ – D♭ – G♭ – C♭.

Keys next to each other on the circle share six of seven notes — they are **closely related**. This is the map you will use for modulation later.

<Composer title="Choosing keys for an orchestra">
Strings resonate best in **sharp keys** (D, A, G, E) because their open strings (G D A E, and C on violas and cellos) ring sympathetically. Brass and winds are often more comfortable in **flat keys** (B♭, E♭, F). Epic film cues frequently sit in D minor, C minor or E♭ — a balance between the sections.
