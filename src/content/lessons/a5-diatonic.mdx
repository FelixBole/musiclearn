Build a triad on every degree of a major scale, using **only notes from that scale**, and you get the seven **diatonic chords** of the key. This is the palette most music draws from.

## Roman numerals

We label diatonic chords by scale degree with **Roman numerals**:

- **UPPERCASE** = major: **I, IV, V**
- **lowercase** = minor: **ii, iii, vi**
- **°** = diminished: **vii°**

| Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C major | C | Dm | Em | F | G | Am | B° |
| G major | G | Am | Bm | C | D | Em | F♯° |

This pattern is identical in **every** major key. Learn it once and you can name every chord in every key.

The point of Roman numerals: **I–vi–IV–V** describes a progression's *function*, independent of key. When you find a progression you love in a reference track, write it down in numerals and you can reuse it anywhere.

## In minor keys

In minor (using harmonic minor for the V): **i – ii° – III – iv – V – VI – vii°**. In A minor: Am, B°, C, Dm, **E** (with G♯), F, G♯°. Many epic progressions also use the natural **v** (Em) and **VII** (G).
