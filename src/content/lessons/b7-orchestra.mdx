The rules of four-part writing exist to keep lines **clear and independent**. An orchestra is, at heart, many such lines — so SATB is the skeleton of most orchestral textures.

## A string section is a choir

| SATB voice | String section |
|---|---|
| Soprano | Violin I |
| Alto | Violin II |
| Tenor | Viola |
| Bass | Cello (+ Double bass an octave lower) |

<Progression romans="I vi IV V I" tonic="D" label="A chorale — imagine it on strings" />

Write the chorale above for strings and it already works: each section has a smooth, playable line. The basses doubling the cellos an octave lower *don't* create parallel octaves in the rule sense — that's **doubling one line**, not two independent voices.

## Brass chorales

Horns and trombones in four-part close harmony is *the* sound of solemn or heroic film moments. Brass punish bad voice leading (parallels and wide leaps stick out), so the SATB rules apply strictly. Low brass sound best with **open** spacing (root–5th–octave at the bottom).

## Doubling across the orchestra

In a big tutti, the same four-part harmony is **doubled** in many octaves:

- **Melody** (soprano): violins I + flutes (+ trumpet for power)
- **Inner voices** (alto/tenor): violins II, violas, horns, clarinets
- **Bass**: cellos + basses + bassoons + trombones/tuba

The rules about doubling (don't double the leading tone, keep the 3rd present) still matter: a leading tone doubled in three octaves in the brass sounds shrill and wants to resolve in parallel octaves.

## When the rules are the colour

- **Planing**: moving entire chords in parallel (e.g. parallel major triads or open 5ths) — epic, medieval, impressionist. Used constantly in fantasy scores.
- **Power 5ths**: low brass and strings in parallel 5ths and octaves for raw, primal weight.
- **Pedal points**: one held note while harmonies change above it — breaks “every voice moves” on purpose to build tension.

<Composer title="A practical workflow">
1. Sketch the harmony as a 4-part chorale on piano (or in this app's drills).
2. Check it: no accidental parallels between independent lines, good spacing, tendency tones resolved.
3. Assign voices to sections, then **double** for weight and colour.
4. Only then break rules deliberately — planing, power 5ths, pedals — where you want that effect.

This turns orchestration from trial-and-error into a decision tree.
</Composer>
